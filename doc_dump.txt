[5] (Style: abstract) | Abstract. Artificial intelligence (AI) and computer vision have enabled the development of intelligent fitness applications capable of analyzing human movements and providing realtime feedback. However, conventional fitness applications primarily focus on repetition counting and provide limited assessment of exercise form. This paper presents FitLens, an intelligent, vision-based exercise coaching system designed to perform real-time pose estimation, repetition counting, exercise form assessment, and personalized feedback using a webcam. The proposed system utilizes MediaPipe PoseLandmarker to extract 33 body landmarks and applies temporal smoothing, confidence-based filtering, and kinematic analysis to improve movement tracking stability. Exercise-specific state machines are employed to identify movement phases, validate repetitions, and detect incorrect exercise form. The system supports six exercises: bicep curls, shoulder presses, push-ups, squats, lunges, and planks. An experimental evaluation involving 15 participants and 90 exercise trials is designed to assess the system under varying camera angles, distances, lighting conditions, and movement speeds. Performance is measured using counting error, mean absolute error, classification metrics, and plank timer error. The proposed approach aims to provide accessible and reliable exercise guidance while reducing false repetition counts and improving form assessment.

[7] (Style: heading1) | Introduction
[8] (Style: Normal) |  Artificial intelligence (AI) and computer vision are enabling new innovations in fitness applications by allowing systems to analyze human movements and provide real-time exercise guidance. Conventional personal training relies on human supervision to evaluate exercise form, monitor repetitions, and provide corrective feedback. However, access to professional personal trainers may be limited by cost, availability, and geographical constraints. A computer vision-based fitness coaching system can provide an accessible alternative by using a webcam to analyze exercise movements.
[9] (Style: Normal) | 
[10] (Style: Normal) | An effective virtual fitness coach must perform more than basic repetition counting. It should identify the exercise being performed, determine the current movement phase, evaluate the user's posture, count valid repetitions, and provide meaningful feedback. These functions require reliable human pose estimation and biomechanical analysis.

Human pose estimation is a computer vision technique that identifies key points on the human body to estimate posture and movement. The extracted landmarks can be used to calculate joint angles and analyze exercise-specific movement patterns. MediaPipe PoseLandmarker provides 33 body landmarks, including their spatial coordinates and visibility information, enabling real-time pose analysis.

However, pose estimation may be affected by variations in camera placement, lighting conditions, movement speed, and landmark detection confidence. Unstable landmark values can result in incorrect joint-angle calculations, false repetition counts, and unreliable form assessment. Therefore, a robust exercise coaching system requires temporal smoothing, confidence-based filtering, movementphase validation, and initial pose calibration.

To address these challenges, this paper presents FitLens, an intelligent AI-based exercise coaching system that integrates pose estimation, kinematic analysis, repetition counting, and form assessment. The system supports six exercises: bicep curls, shoulder presses, push-ups, squats, lunges, and planks.

The primary contributions of this work are as follows:

[12] (Style: Normal) | 2. Integration of temporal smoothing and confidence based filtering to improve landmark stability. 
[13] (Style: Normal) | 3. Implementation of exercise-specific kinematic rules and state machines for repetition counting and form assessment.

[17] (Style: Normal) | 2.1  Human Pose Estimation 
[18] (Style: Normal) |  Human pose estimation enables computer vision systems to infer human body posture by identifying two-dimensional (2D) or three-dimensional (3D) keypoints from images or video frames. Advances in machine learning and neural network optimization have enabled pose estimation models to operate in real time on consumer devices. OpenPose introduced a widely used approach for multiperson pose estimation, while MoveNet and MediaPipe Pose provide efficient alternatives for real-time applications. MediaPipe PoseLandmarker can operate on a local device and estimate 33 body landmarks for each video frame. Each landmark contains spatial coordinates and visibility information that can be used to evaluate body posture and movement. In FitLens, MediaPipe PoseLandmarker is used to estimate the user's body posture. The extracted landmarks are processed by a Python FastAPI backend, which performs biomechanical analysis and evaluates exercise-specific movement patterns. [3, 4]

[20] (Style: Normal) | 2.2  Exercise Recognition 
[21] (Style: Normal) | Exercise recognition involves identifying the type of physical activity being performed based on body posture and movement characteristics. In pose-based fitness applications, joint angles and the relative positions of body landmarks can be used to distinguish between exercise movements. For example, bicep curls primarily involve elbow flexion, whereas squats involve coordinated movement of the hips, knees, and ankles. These exercise-specific characteristics can be represented using predefined kinematic rules. FitLens employs a rule-based approach in which the backend evaluates relevant joint angles, movement phases, and posture constraints for each supported exercise. This enables the system to analyze exercise movements without relying exclusively on general-purpose activity classification. [5, 6].
[22] (Style: Normal) | 
[23] (Style: Normal) | 2.3  Exercise Repetition 
[24] (Style: Normal) | Counting Repetition counting is a fundamental component of automated fitness coaching. A basic approach counts repetitions whenever a joint angle crosses a predefined threshold. However, this method may produce false counts when landmark fluctuations or unintended movements cause repeated threshold crossings. More advanced approaches use state machines to represent different phases of an exercise. A repetition is counted only when the movement follows a valid sequence of states. Additional conditions, such as movement direction, posture validation, and temporal stability, can further reduce false counts. FitLens uses exercise-specific state machines to identify movement phases and validate repetitions. This approach allows the system to distinguish between partial movements, valid repetitions, and unintended movements.
[25] (Style: Normal) | 
[26] (Style: Normal) | 2.4  Exercise Form Assessment 
[27] (Style: Normal) | Exercise form assessment involves evaluating whether a movement follows the expected biomechanical pattern. Incorrect posture, incomplete movement, and improper joint alignment may reduce exercise effectiveness and increase the risk of discomfort or injury. Pose estimation provides body landmarks that can be used to calculate joint angles and assess posture. Exercisespecific rules can then identify deviations from expected movement patterns. FitLens evaluates exercise form using kinematic constraints, joint-angle thresholds, and posture-related conditions. The assessment is integrated with repetition counting to prevent incorrectly performed movements from being treated as valid repetitions when the relevant form constraints are not satisfied.

[29] (Style: Normal) | 2.5  Personalized Training 
[30] (Style: Normal) | Personalized training systems use information about an individual's exercise performance to provide relevant feedback and recommendations. Repetition counts, form scores, joint angles, and detected movement errors can be used to identify areas for improvement. FitLens provides exercise-related feedback based on its movement analysis and form assessment outputs. The system is designed to support personalized coaching by using session information and detected form errors to guide users toward improved exercise performance. The current implementation focuses on real-time movement analysis and feedback. A detailed adaptive recommendation algorithm based on long-term user history remains a potential area for further development.

[46] (Style: Normal) | 4        PROPOSED SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE 
[47] (Style: Normal) | 
[48] (Style: Normal) | FitLens is an end-to-end exercise coaching system that converts webcam video into exercise-related feedback. The proposed architecture consists of camera input, video processing, pose estimation, stability checking, adaptive smoothing, joint-angle calculation, movement-state identification, form assessment, repetition counting, and feedback generation. The system initially captures video through a webcam and extracts body landmarks using MediaPipe PoseLandmarker. The landmarks are then filtered and smoothed before being used for biomechanical analysis. The backend evaluates exercise-specific movement rules and determines whether a valid repetition or posture has been detected. 
[49] (Style: Normal) |  Exercise Video Processing 
[50] (Style: Normal) | The video processing stage captures frames from the user's webcam and prepares them for pose estimation. The system performs preliminary quality checks to identify unsuitable input conditions. Lighting quality is evaluated using the luminance equation: Y=0.299R+0.587G+0.114B(1)Y = 0.299R + 0.587G + 0.114B \tag{1} where RR, GG, and BB represent the red, green, and blue color components, respectively. A low-luminance warning is generated when the calculated luminance falls below the configured threshold of 35. This helps notify users when lighting conditions may negatively affect landmark detection. The system also filters body landmarks using their visibility values. Landmarks with visibility below 0.40 are excluded from further analysis. In addition, a two-second initial calibration period is used to establish a stable starting pose and reduce false movement detection at the beginning of an exercise session.
[51] (Style: Normal) |  Pose Estimation Methods 
[52] (Style: Normal) | MediaPipe PoseLandmarker estimates 33 body landmarks for each processed video frame. Each landmark is represented as: Li=(xi,yi,zi,vi)(2)L_i = (x_i,y_i,z_i,v_i) \tag{2} where xix_i and yiy_i represent normalized image coordinates, ziz_i represents relative depth, and viv_i represents the landmark visibility or confidence value. The extracted landmarks are used to determine body posture and calculate exercise-specific joint angles. However, raw landmark coordinates may fluctuate due to detection noise and rapid movement. To reduce these fluctuations, FitLens applies a velocityadaptive exponential moving average (EMA): L^t(i)=αtLt(i)+(1−αt)L^t−1(i)(3)\hat{L}_t^{(i)} = \alpha_t L_t^{(i)} + (1-\alpha_t)\hat{L}_{t-1}^{(i)} \tag{3} where Lt(i)L_t^{(i)} is the current landmark, L^t−1(i)\hat{L}_{t-1}^{(i)} is its previously smoothed value, and αt\alpha_t is the adaptive smoothing coefficient. The smoothing coefficient is adjusted according to movement conditions: • αt=0.35\alpha_t = 0.35 for micro-jitter reduction. • αt=0.85\alpha_t = 0.85 for rapid movement. • αt=0.65\alpha_t = 0.65 under normal movement conditions. This adaptive approach is intended to reduce minor fluctuations while preserving responsiveness during faster movements. The browser sends the extracted landmark data to the Python FastAPI backend for movement analysis. To control processing frequency, inference requests are throttled to approximately 80 ms intervals, corresponding to about 12.5 requests per second.

[58] (Style: Normal) | The calculated angles are used to assess the six supported exercises.
[59] (Style: Normal) |  1) Bicep Curl: The system tracks the shoulder, elbow, and wrist landmarks. The elbow angle is used to identify the downward and upward phases. The downward phase corresponds to an elbow angle of at least 140∘140^\circ, while the upward phase corresponds to an angle of at most 50∘50^\circ. The system also evaluates whether the elbows remain close to the torso. 
[60] (Style: Normal) | 2) Shoulder Press: The system tracks the elbow angles of both arms. The downward phase corresponds to an elbow angle of at most 90∘90^\circ, while the upward phase requires an angle of at least 155∘155^\circ. Bilateral symmetry is assessed by ensuring that the difference between the left and right elbow angles does not exceed 25∘25^\circ.
[61] (Style: Normal) |  3) Push-up: The system tracks elbow flexion and spine alignment. The top phase corresponds to an elbow angle greater than 150∘150^\circ, while the bottom phase corresponds to an angle of at most 95∘95^\circ. Spine alignment is assessed using the shoulder, hip, and ankle landmarks to identify excessive hip sag or piking. 
[62] (Style: Normal) | 4) Squat: The system tracks the hip, knee, and ankle landmarks to determine squat depth. The standing phase corresponds to a knee angle greater than 155∘155^\circ, while the deep squat phase corresponds to an angle of at most 100∘100^\circ. Additional torso and knee alignment constraints are used to assess movement form.
[63] (Style: Normal) |  5) Lunge: The system evaluates the lead knee angle to identify the standing and lunge phases. The standing phase corresponds to a knee angle greater than 150∘150^\circ, while the lunge phase corresponds to an angle of at most 105∘105^\circ. Additional rules assess lead-leg alignment and knee positioning. 
[64] (Style: Normal) | 6) Plank: The system evaluates spine neutrality and shoulder alignment during a continuous hold. The full-body alignment angle is expected to remain within the configured range of 155∘155^\circ–195∘195^\circ. When form becomes invalid, the system can automatically pause the timer until acceptable posture is restored. The exercise-specific rules allow the system to evaluate movement phases and form constraints rather than relying solely on individual joint-angle threshold crossings. 
[65] (Style: Normal) |  Personalized Training Module 
[66] (Style: Normal) | The personalized training module uses exercise performance information to provide feedback during a workout session. The system generates outputs such as repetition count, movement stage, form score, joint angles, feedback messages, and session metrics. These outputs can be used to communicate detected form errors and support exercise improvement. For example, the system can provide corrective feedback when a movement fails to satisfy the relevant posture constraints. The current implementation primarily focuses on real-time exercise feedback. More advanced personalization, including long-term performance tracking, user-specific progression, and adaptive workout recommendations, can be incorporated in future versions.

[87] (Style: Normal) |  6        DISCUSSION AND LIMITATIONS 
[88] (Style: Normal) | 
[89] (Style: Normal) | FitLens integrates multiple processing techniques to improve the stability of exercise analysis. Temporal filtering reduces fluctuations in landmark coordinates, while confidence-based filtering excludes landmarks with insufficient visibility. Start-pose calibration helps prevent false movement detection during the initial phase of a session. The use of exercise-specific state machines allows the system to identify valid movement sequences rather than relying on a single threshold. This is particularly important for exercises involving multiple movement phases, such as push-ups, where both the top and bottom positions must be identified before a repetition can be validated. Despite these mechanisms, system performance may be affected by environmental and movement-related factors. Poor lighting can reduce landmark detection reliability, while excessive camera distance may make it difficult to capture relevant body landmarks. Camera angle can also affect the apparent joint angles used for biomechanical analysis. The system is designed to provide warnings under insufficient lighting conditions. However, extreme lighting conditions and partial body occlusion may still result in unstable pose estimation. Similarly, rapid or explosive movements may introduce additional landmark fluctuations, potentially affecting movement-phase identification. The current implementation supports six exercises and relies on predefined kinematic rules. These rules may not fully represent individual differences in body proportions, flexibility, exercise technique, or physical ability. Consequently, the system may require further calibration and validation before it can provide reliable feedback across a wider range of users and exercise variations. Another limitation is that the current personalization module primarily provides feedback based on detected exercise performance. Advanced personalization using long-term user history, adaptive difficulty, and individualized training progression remains an area for future development. Finally, the proposed evaluation uses a limited participant cohort. The results may not generalize to all users, camera devices, or environmental conditions. Larger and more diverse studies are required to assess the system's robustness and practical effectiveness.

[91] (Style: Normal) | 7   CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK 
[92] (Style: Normal) | 
[93] (Style: Normal) | This paper presented FitLens, an intelligent AI-based system for real-time exercise form analysis, repetition counting, and personalized feedback. The system integrates MediaPipe PoseLandmarker, temporal smoothing, confidence-based filtering, joint-angle calculation, and exercise-specific state machines to analyze human movements using a webcam. FitLens supports six exercises: bicep curls, shoulder presses, push-ups, squats, lunges, and planks. Its architecture combines pose estimation and biomechanical rules to identify movement phases, assess exercise form, count valid repetitions, and provide feedback. The system also incorporates initial pose calibration and input-quality checks to improve movement tracking stability. An experimental evaluation framework involving 15 participants and 90 exercise trials is designed to assess the system under different camera angles, distances, lighting conditions, and movement speeds. Counting Error, Mean Absolute Error, classification metrics, and plank timer error are used to evaluate system performance. The evaluation also compares the proposed pipeline with simpler repetitioncounting approaches. Future work will focus on supporting a wider range of exercises, improving robustness to body-part occlusion, and incorporating user-specific range-of-motion calibration. Additional research may investigate the use of depth cameras and more advanced personalization methods to improve movement analysis and provide individualized training recommendations. The proposed system establishes a foundation for accessible, webcam-based exercise coaching. Its effectiveness in practical fitness environments will be further assessed through the completion of the planned experiments and analysis of the resulting performance metrics.
